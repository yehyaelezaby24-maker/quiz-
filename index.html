1) Introduction and Basics

  • Puerperium: the 6 weeks after childbirth, when the uterus and genital tract return to the non-pregnant state. The raw placental site and any tears are open doors for bacteria during this time.
  • Puerperal infection / sepsis: infection that occurs when a new mother gets an infection related to giving birth. It is the 6th leading cause of death among new mothers.
  • Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) is the leading cause of maternal mortality; its rate rose from 1.5% (1999) to 4.1% (2009).

2) Definition and Etiology

Definition: infection of the genital tract during or after labor.

Causative organisms

  1. Anaerobic organisms: produce mild infection. They are non-pathogenic but become pathogenic in the presence of dead tissue (e.g., retained placenta), because dead tissue has no blood supply and little oxygen, which suits anaerobes.
  2. Group A hemolytic streptococci: produce severe infection. They spread rapidly and cause septicemia.
  3. Staphylococci: usually present in infected perineal wounds (skin organisms).
  4. Others: E. coli (from the bowel/perineum) and non-hemolytic streptococci.

Modes of infection

ModeSource
ExogenousFrom outside: the attendants or instruments, e.g., hemolytic streptococci.
EndogenousThe organism is already present in the vagina (e.g., anaerobic streptococci) and becomes pathogenic if there is a predisposing factor, e.g., dead tissue.
AutogenousThe organism spreads by blood from an aseptic (septic) focus elsewhere in the body, e.g., tonsils.

3) Predisposing Factors

Factors in the genital tract

  • Lack of antiseptic measures.
  • Instrumental delivery (Ventouse/vacuum, forceps extraction) and intrauterine maneuvers (e.g., manual removal of placenta): they carry organisms upward and cause tissue trauma.
  • Retained placental parts: dead tissue + bleeding = ideal culture medium.
  • Laceration of the genital tract.
  • Premature rupture of membranes (PROM) or prolonged labor: the barrier between vagina and uterus is lost, so organisms ascend. Cesarean birth also adds a surgical wound.
  • Repeated PV (vaginal examinations).

General factors

  • Diabetes, anemia or low immunity (weaker defense and slower healing).
  • Presence of a septic focus (e.g., tonsillitis, which gives autogenous infection).

4) Pathology

A) Primary sites of infection

1. Genital tract lacerations (perineal or cervical): the wound is red, hot, painful and swollen, with greenish-yellow discharge (pus).
REEDA (wound assessment): Redness, Edema, Ecchymosis (bruising), Discharge, Approximation (are wound edges closed together?).
2. The uterus (the most common site)

A. Putrid endometritis (putrid = foul-smelling, from decaying tissue): a mild type due to low-virulence organisms and good patient resistance. The uterus is subinvoluted (boggy: soft, not shrinking back) and its cavity contains a large amount of necrotic tissue and pus. The muscle next to the endometrium contains a leucocytic barrier that limits spread of infection.

B. Septic endometritis: a severe type due to virulent organisms such as hemolytic streptococci. The uterus itself is involved, the cavity is lined by a pyogenic membrane (pus-forming layer), and the leucocytic barrier is lacking, which favours spread of infection.

⚠️ The slide says "avirulent" for septic endometritis; it is most likely a typo for virulent.
💡 الفكرة: Putrid = عدوى موضعية جوه الرحم محبوسة بحاجز. Septic = الحاجز مش موجود فالعدوى تهرب للدم والأنسجة.

B) Secondary sites of infection (spread)

  1. Parametritis: due to direct or lymphatic spread from cervical or vaginal vault tears. The exudate starts at the base of the broad ligament and, when excessive, pushes the uterus to the opposite side. A parametric abscess may develop and point to: the vagina, rectum or bladder, the buttocks, the thigh, or the peri-renal space.
  2. Salpingo-oophoritis (tubes and ovaries): due to ascent along the lymphatics from the primary sites.
  3. Pelvic thrombophlebitis: starts in the uterine veins, then spreads to the pelvic or femoral veins. Infection damages the vein wall and slows blood flow, so a clot forms.
  4. Peritonitis: due to direct spread from salpingitis, an infected cesarean wound or lacerations in the uterus. It is either pelvic peritonitis (i.e., pelvic abscess) or generalized.
⚠️ Generalized spread: septicemia and septic shock result from severe infection.

5) Diagnosis

Symptoms

  • Pain: suprapubic/pelvic; generalized abdominal pain and tenderness in generalized peritonitis; pain in the lower limbs in cases of DVT.
  • Discharge: in putrid endometritis the lochia is excessive and offensive.
  • Bleeding: i.e., secondary postpartum hemorrhage (after 24 hours of delivery), because infected uterus cannot contract well.
  • Fever, headache and malaise.
  • Vomiting: continuous if generalized peritonitis develops.
  • Diarrhea and rectal tenesmus (painful urge to defecate) in localized peritonitis, i.e., pelvic abscess, because the abscess in the pouch of Douglas irritates the rectum (proctitis).

Examination: general

  • Fever and tachycardia.
  • Dehydration in generalized peritonitis due to paralytic ileus.
  • In septicemia: 1. Rigors. 2. Tachycardia out of proportion to the fever. 3. Flushed, warm, dry skin. 4. Jaundice and cyanosis.

Differential diagnosis (other causes of fever after delivery)

  1. History: pre-existing infections before labor such as renal or chest infection; sore throat, burning micturition and mastalgia (breast pain); diseases lowering resistance (diabetes, anemia); mode of labor (spontaneous or instrumental).
  2. General examination: vital signs (pulse, temperature, blood pressure); cyanosis, jaundice and purpura if septicemia; the throat (pharyngitis, tonsillitis); the breast (mastitis) and the chest (chest infection); the lower limbs (deep vein thrombosis).
  3. Abdominal: tenderness and rigidity; subinvolution of the uterus; the renal angle for pyelonephritis.
  4. Vaginal: the lochia for amount and odor; infected laceration in perineum, vagina or cervix; uterus for subinvolution and tenderness; pelvic mass: (a) parametric abscess is felt lateral to the uterus, (b) pelvic abscess is usually felt through the posterior fornix.

Investigations

  • A swab from the vagina and cervix for aerobic cultures.
  • Urine analysis, culture and sensitivity.
  • Full blood picture (CBC).
  • Blood culture at the height of the fever (bacteria are most numerous in blood then).
  • Doppler ultrasound for DVT.
  • Others, e.g., chest X-ray and blood film for malaria.

6) Treatment

A) Prevention

  1. During pregnancy (antenatal): improve the general condition; treat diabetes and anemia; treat any septic focus; avoid coitus in the last month of pregnancy.
  2. During labor: proper antiseptic measures; care with instrumental delivery and intrauterine manipulation; avoid retained fragments (D&C = dilatation and curettage, i.e., dilating the cervix and scraping out retained tissue); suture genital tract wounds within 24 hours; antibiotics for premature rupture of membranes; avoid prolonged labor; avoid repeated PV.
  3. During puerperium: maintain aseptic precautions; minimize visitors and prevent those with sore throat; isolate suspected and infected cases.

B) Definitive treatment

  1. General measures: isolation in a fever hospital or separate room; highly nutritive diet and vitamins; observation of clinical manifestations and laboratory data.
  2. Antibiotics: a combination is started: Ampicillin + Gentamicin + Metronidazole (together they cover aerobic and anaerobic organisms). They are changed according to results of culture and sensitivity.
  3. Symptomatic: analgesics and antipyretics.
  4. Promotion of drainage: Fowler's position (so pus collects in the pelvis, away from the upper abdomen); Ergometrine to help expulsion of retained placental parts or membranes (it contracts the uterus); removal of sutures in infected wounds; drainage of pelvic abscess by posterior colpotomy (incision in the posterior vaginal wall); drainage of parametric abscess where it points.
  5. Treatment of complications (septic shock, DVT, peritonitis).

7) Nursing Care

Nursing diagnosis (2 actual, 1 potential): infection may be related to broken skin and/or traumatized tissues or chronic disease.
Outcomes: verbalized understanding of individual causative risk factors; initiates behaviors to limit spread of infection and reduce risk factors; achieves timely healing, free of additional complication.

Nursing interventions

  1. Review prenatal, intrapartal and postpartal record.
  2. Demonstrate and maintain strict hand-washing policy for staff, client and visitors.
  3. Provide for and instruct the client in proper disposal of contaminated linens, dressings and peripads; maintain isolation if indicated.
  4. Encourage correct perineal cleaning after voiding and defecation and frequent changing of peripads.
  5. Demonstrate proper fundal massage.
  6. Monitor temperature, pulse and respirations.
  7. Observe the perineum/incision for other signs of infection (redness, edema, ecchymosis).
  8. Monitor oral/parenteral intake, stressing at least 2000 ml fluid per day; note urine output, degree of dehydration, nausea, vomiting or diarrhea.
  9. Encourage moist heat (sitz baths) and dry heat from perineal lights for 15 min, 2-4 times daily.
  10. Arrange transfer to an intensive care setting as appropriate.
